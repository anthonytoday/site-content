You already know where this breaks

Not "you're disorganized." Every firm your size has these five. What they actually cost is the part nobody puts a number against.

  • An investor or lender asks for an update Days of principal time assembling something that should take an hour. Do that quarterly and it is a working fortnight a year, billed to nobody.
  • A diligence request lands The documents exist. Nobody can produce them on demand. A slow data room gets read as a signal about everything else you manage.
  • A date moves and nobody sees it Financing windows, option dates and lease breaks do not reschedule themselves. One missed date on one deal costs more than this entire engagement.
  • The person who knew where everything lived leaves Institutional memory walks out with them, and you rebuild it from old inboxes at your own hourly rate.
  • Operations are still manual when you raise or sell Diligence findings turn into price adjustments. A buyer pricing in key-person risk and undocumented process is repricing the firm, not a line item.

None of these are urgent on an ordinary Tuesday. They are only expensive on the day someone is looking at you, and that is never a day you get to choose.

How the 30 days run

Two phases, one engagement. The first ten days exist so that the decision to spend the rest is made against something real rather than a proposal.

Days 1 to 10 Foundation

Your firm, live on your own deals

I take workspace access on day one and model how your firm actually transacts. Not a generic real estate template, and not a slide deck describing one. By day ten you are looking at your own deals moving through your own stages.

  • Deal structure modelled on how you transact. Your stages, your deal types, your terminology. Acquisition, development and hold do not share a pipeline unless you run them that way.
  • Live pipeline and summary views using your own records. A handful of real deals, loaded so you recognise the business rather than a demo.
  • Three roles configured and demonstrated working. Principal, team and external. You watch a guest hit "No access" rather than read that they would.
  • A written gap list. Exactly what days 11 to 30 would add, itemised, so the decision at day ten is specific.
Not in this phase, by design: no migration, no sensitive or investor data, no team training. Ten days is enough to prove the architecture on a slice of your business. It is not enough to move a firm, and pretending otherwise is how these projects fail.
Days 11 to 30 Full build

Everything moved in and handed over

The second phase is the labour and the change management. This is where the workspace stops being a good idea and becomes the place the firm actually works.

  • Migration, inventoried and mapped. What lives in Drive, in email and in the folder nobody admits to, moved deliberately rather than dumped.
  • Full architecture across deals, assets, documents and portfolio. Relations and rollups so a number appears once and reports everywhere.
  • Access model implemented and tested. Confidentiality enforced by where a page lives, never by a filter, because a filter is not a permission. Five written tests you run yourself to prove it holds.
  • Investor reporting. An update that assembles in minutes instead of costing you days of principal time every quarter.
  • Training, two tracks. One for you, one for the team. Different jobs need different sessions.
  • Handover to a named owner. A person on your side, a maintenance routine and full documentation, so the system survives me leaving.
